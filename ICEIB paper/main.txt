AirGuard AI: An Evidence-Grounded Domain AI Agent for Station-Scale Short-Term
PM2.5 Forecasting and Conservative Outdoor Activity Decision Support

Ming-Hsien Tsai (7115056013), Hung-Yang Lin (7115056179), and Ping-Kun Hsu (8114056002)
AIoT Data Analytics and Applications (AIoT-DA) Research Group, Team 2
Taichung, Taiwan
Repository: https://github.com/sam052003/AirGuard-AI | https://github.com/sam052003/AIoT_DA_1111

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Abstract—Ambient air pollution remains a major global environmental health risk.
According to the World Health Organization (WHO), outdoor air pollution caused
approximately 4.2 million premature deaths worldwide in 2019. In Taiwan, data
from the Ministry of Environment (MOENV) show that the national annual mean fine
particulate matter (PM2.5) concentration declined from 16.2 ug/m^3 in 2019 to
12.8 ug/m^3 in 2025, representing an approximate 21% long-term improvement;
however, macro-level annual averages do not directly resolve station-specific,
short-term exposure risks when citizens schedule outdoor activities. Existing
public monitoring dashboards primarily display current air quality status while
leaving users to manually reconcile short-term concentration trajectories,
activity duration, population vulnerability, data freshness, and predictive
uncertainty. Conversely, unconstrained large language models (LLMs) risk
hallucinating environmental metrics or conflating single-pollutant predictions
with official multi-pollutant Air Quality Index (AQI) ratings. To address this
gap, this paper proposes AirGuard AI, an evidence-grounded Domain AI Agent for
station-scale short-term PM2.5 forecasting and conservative outdoor activity
decision support. Using six official MOENV monitoring stations in Taichung City
as a pilot domain, AirGuard AI integrates four official open datasets (AQX_P_432
real-time AQI, AQX_P_13 hourly PM2.5 observations, AQX_P_488 historical AQI
records, and AQX_P_07 TWD97 station coordinates) with versioned MOENV activity
guidelines. The analytics layer benchmarks Ridge regression, Random Forest, and
XGBoost against Persistence and 24-hour Seasonal Naive baselines across 1-, 3-,
and 6-hour forecast horizons under chronological splitting (70/15/15) and
rolling-origin evaluation, reporting Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean
Square Error (RMSE), and Skill Score. A tool-allowlisted ReAct planner agent
interprets user activity contexts, invokes deterministic data and model tools,
verifies timestamp provenance, compares eligible candidate activity windows, and
computes a station-scale concentration-time exposure index (ug*h/m^3) alongside
explicit uncertainty bounds. We report the completed Phase W1 empirical data
quality engineering and cryptographic SHA256 snapshot verification across
131,160 retrieved hourly slots from the six Taichung stations (2024-2026), and
detail a 30-scenario dual-track validation protocol covering agent tool
selection, citation fidelity, regulatory compliance, and fail-closed degradation
when data are stale or differences fall within model error bounds.

Index Terms—Air Quality Index (AQI), Decision Support Systems, Domain AI Agent,
Explainable AI (SHAP), Internet of Things (IoT) Open Data, PM2.5 Time-Series
Forecasting, ReAct Tool Calling.

I. INTRODUCTION

A. Motivation and Public Health Context
Ambient air pollution continues to pose a persistent threat to human health and
daily outdoor mobility. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that in
2019, 99% of the global population resided in areas failing to meet WHO air
quality guideline levels, with ambient (outdoor) air pollution accounting for
approximately 4.2 million premature deaths worldwide [1], [2]. In Taiwan,
regulatory mitigation efforts documented by the Ministry of Environment (MOENV)
have reduced the national annual mean concentration of fine particulate matter
with an aerodynamic diameter of 2.5 um or less (PM2.5) from 16.2 ug/m^3 in 2019
to 12.8 ug/m^3 in 2025, an overall improvement of roughly 21% [3], [4]. Despite
this encouraging macro-scale trend, an annual national average operates on a
fundamentally different spatiotemporal scale than an individual's immediate
daily decision, such as determining which 40-minute window over the next three
hours is comparatively safer for outdoor jogging or commuting near a specific
monitoring station. Diurnal meteorology, local traffic emissions, and seasonal
transboundary episodes still induce rapid hour-to-hour fluctuations that require
short-term, localized, and context-aware decision interpretation.

B. Application Background and Spatiotemporal Granularity
To support public environmental awareness without requiring proprietary sensor
deployments, Taiwan's MOENV Open Data Platform publishes station-level real-time
Air Quality Index (AQI) reports (dataset AQX_P_432), hourly pollutant
observations updated daily (AQX_P_13), historical AQI and meteorological series
(AQX_P_488), and official monitoring station metadata with TWD97 coordinates
(AQX_P_07) [5]-[8]. For general exercisers, outdoor workers, and sensitive
subpopulations (e.g., children, older adults, and individuals with respiratory
or cardiovascular conditions), a practical query is rarely a static lookup of
"What is the current AQI?" Instead, users seek forward-looking comparative
guidance: "Given my sensitivity group and a planned 40-minute outdoor activity
near Xitun Station in Taichung within the next six hours, which candidate time
window carries lower station-scale exposure, and how reliable is the underlying
data?" Answering such queries requires bridging raw IoT environmental open data,
short-term time-series forecasting, regulatory health guidance, and transparent
uncertainty communication at the monitoring-station scale.

C. Problem Definition and Existing Limitations
Transforming public air quality data into trustworthy activity recommendations
faces four structural limitations in existing literature and consumer platforms:

1) Spatiotemporal and Provenance Ambiguity: Public dashboards and third-party
applications frequently conflate observation time (observed_at), official release
time (published_at), and client retrieval time (retrieved_at). Furthermore,
upstream sensor maintenance or transmission dropouts produce missing hourly
slots that naive pipelines erroneously impute as zero concentration (0 ug/m^3),
falsely signaling clean air. Equally problematic is spatial over-extrapolation:
fixed regulatory stations characterize regional or neighborhood-scale ambient
air, which cannot independently validate street-by-street microenvironmental
gradients or "cleanest route" navigation rankings without co-located mobile
monitoring [9], [10].

2) Regulatory Semantic Divergence Between AQI and PM2.5: Under Taiwan's MOENV
AQI standard (including the revised sub-index concentration thresholds effective
January 1, 2025), the official AQI is a composite maximum sub-index derived
across six criteria pollutants (O3, PM2.5, PM10, CO, SO2, and NO2), each
governed by distinct moving-average time windows [11]. Predicting future raw
hourly PM2.5 concentrations and presenting them as "predicted official AQI"
violates regulatory semantics and misleads end users.

3) Forecasting Pitfalls and the Accuracy-Decision Gap: Many machine learning
(ML) studies on PM2.5 prediction rely on random train-test splits or future-
looking rolling statistics that introduce temporal data leakage, inflating
reported accuracy while failing to benchmark against simple yet strong baselines
such as Persistence and Seasonal Naive [12]-[14]. Moreover, recent empirical
evidence in environmental decision planning demonstrates that a forecast model
achieving a lower overall Mean Absolute Error (MAE) does not automatically yield
superior decisions regarding whether or when to schedule outdoor activities,
particularly when candidate time windows differ by less than the model's
validation error or when peak pollution episodes are smoothed out [15].

4) Unconstrained Generative AI Risks: While Large Language Models (LLMs) excel
at parsing natural language intent, deploying an unconstrained conversational
agent over environmental APIs introduces severe reliability hazards, including
numeric hallucination, silent use of stale cache snapshots as live observations,
susceptibility to indirect prompt injection embedded in external payloads, and
unauthorized medical diagnosis [16]-[18].

D. Proposed Innovations and Expected Contributions
To overcome these limitations, this paper presents AirGuard AI (v3 specification),
an evidence-grounded Domain AI Agent designed around strict tool allowlisting,
dual-track separation of official AQI and predicted hourly PM2.5, and fail-
closed degradation. Rather than treating the system as an unconstrained chatbot
or an isolated black-box regressor, AirGuard AI enforces a verifiable contract
across data ingestion, short-term forecasting, regulatory rule lookup, and
conservative window comparison. The primary contributions of this work are:

1) Reproducible MOENV Data Engineering and Cryptographic Provenance (Verified in
Phase W1): We design and empirically validate a Python ingestion and quality-
control pipeline over four MOENV datasets across all six official monitoring
stations in Taichung City (Fengyuan, Shalu, Dali, Zhongming, Xitun, and Taiwan
Avenue). The pipeline resolves upstream duplicate daily records, trailing date
whitespace, and unobserved calendar days while preserving raw paginated API
bytes with SHA256 manifests for deterministic offline replay.

2) Leakage-Safe Multi-Horizon PM2.5 Benchmarking Protocol: We establish a
chronological 70/15/15 split and rolling-origin evaluation framework comparing
Persistence and 24-hour Seasonal Naive baselines against Ridge regression,
Random Forest, and XGBoost across h in {1, 3, 6} hours on identical valid sample
pairs, explicitly annotating historical backtests lacking publication-delay logs
as reconstructed_from_observed_at.

3) Three-Tier Separation of Agent Planning, Analytics, and Domain Rules: We
architect an eight-module workflow where a ReAct-based planner agent is restricted
to an eight-tool allowlist. Official AQI values, short-term PM2.5 forecasts,
and deterministic station-scale concentration-time exposure proxies (ug*h/m^3)
are rendered in separate provenance cards with uncertainty-aware abstention when
inter-window differences fall within model error margins.

4) Dual-Track Verification and Boundary Governance: We formulate a comprehensive
evaluation design pairing Track A quantitative time-series metrics (MAE, RMSE,
Skill Score) with a Track B 30-scenario test suite auditing tool selection,
citation completeness, rule compliance, prompt-injection defense, and graceful
degradation, while explicitly excluding unverified street-level routing and
personal inhaled dose claims.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related
work across AI forecasting/agents and environmental exposure domain knowledge,
synthesizing key professional controversies and the research gap. Section III
details the proposed eight-module AirGuard AI architecture, data schemas,
forecasting protocol, and agent algorithms. Section IV presents the empirical
Phase W1 data audit for Taichung City alongside the dual-track evaluation
framework. Section V concludes the paper and outlines ongoing phases.


II. RELATED WORK AND DOMAIN RESEARCH

Following an interdisciplinary methodology balancing artificial intelligence
techniques with environmental health domain know-how, we review prior literature
across short-term pollutant forecasting, explainable ML, tool-augmented language
agents, and air pollution exposure science.

A. AI Technology: Time-Series Forecasting, Explainability, and Tool Agents
Recent large-scale syntheses emphasize that air quality forecasting performance
is highly contingent on validation regime and baseline rigor. Chianese and
Riccio [12] conducted a systematic mapping of 409 air-quality forecasting
studies, concluding that no single model family universally dominates across
pollutants, forecast horizons, and sensor network densities; rather, evaluation
design and temporal leakage prevention are decisive determinants of real-world
validity. Similarly, Joseph and Ganapathy [19] reviewed statistical, classical
ML, and deep learning paradigms for PM2.5 time-series forecasting, noting that
regularized linear models and gradient-boosted decision trees frequently rival
complex deep neural networks on tabular station histories while offering lower
inference latency and simpler operational maintenance. Hewamalage et al. [13]
and Lu and Lu [14] systematically cataloged common pitfalls in data science
forecasting—specifically random k-fold cross-validation on autocorrelated
series, global feature scaling prior to splitting, and omitting naive baselines—
advocating rolling-origin evaluation and skill scores relative to Persistence.
Lessons from the M5 forecasting competition [20] further reinforce that complex
models must demonstrate consistent gains over seasonal and persistence benchmarks
under identical sample windows before deployment.

To enhance model transparency, Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [21] has
been widely adopted in environmental modeling. However, in a systematic review
of interpretable ML for air pollution, Houdou et al. [22] cautioned that post-hoc
feature attribution quantifies marginal contributions within a fitted model
rather than physical atmospheric causality; consequently, high SHAP importance
for wind speed or lagged PM2.5 must not be communicated to lay users as a causal
proof of emission sources.

In parallel, the ReAct (Reasoning and Acting) paradigm introduced by Yao et al.
[16] demonstrated that interleaving verbal reasoning traces with external tool
invocations substantially reduces factual hallucination in multi-step tasks. In
the environmental domain, Gao et al. [23] explored an Instructor-Worker LLM
architecture for wildfire air quality analysis, while Nie and Liu [17] evaluated
LLMs for environmental decision-making, concluding that language models should
operate as supervised assistants constrained by deterministic domain models
rather than autonomous decision-makers. Furthermore, the OWASP Top 10 for LLM
Applications (2026) [18] highlights Prompt Injection (LLM01) and Excessive
Agency as primary security risks whenever agents ingest external data payloads,
mandating strict schema validation, tool allowlisting, and output verification.

B. Domain Know-How: Exposure Science, Regulatory AQI, and Decision Utility
From an environmental health perspective, the WHO Global Air Quality Guidelines
[1] establish evidence-based reference thresholds for short- and long-term
pollutant concentrations, while emphasizing that ambient guidelines do not
constitute clinical diagnostic thresholds for individual patients. Exposure
assessment literature by Steinle et al. [9] underscores a fundamental hierarchy
distinguishing three quantities: 1) ambient station concentration C(t) measured
at a fixed regulatory monitor (ug/m^3); 2) time-integrated concentration proxy
sum(C(t_i) * Delta_t_i) (ug*h/m^3); and 3) true personal inhaled dose, which
depends on microenvironmental infiltration, street-canyon geometry, physical
exertion, minute ventilation rate, and respiratory protection. Furthermore, the
U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) [10] and Taiwanese spatiotemporal
modeling by Lo et al. [24] illustrate that estimating hyper-local or street-
segment pollution requires dense land-use regression, satellite aerosol optical
depth, or calibrated mobile sensor networks; fixed regulatory stations alone
cannot substantiate street-level clean-route navigation.

At the regulatory level, Taiwan's MOENV AQI framework [11] computes sub-indices
across six pollutants over pollutant-specific averaging windows (e.g., weighted
moving averages for PM2.5 and PM10, 8-hour averages for O3 and CO, and 1-hour
averages for SO2 and NO2) and maps the maximum sub-index to six color-coded
categories accompanied by differentiated activity guidance for the general
public and sensitive groups. Crucially, Berlinghieri et al. [15] recently
evaluated six operational hourly PM2.5 forecast systems during wildfire episodes
to test whether hourly forecasts are sufficiently accurate for daily activity
planning. By separating binary decisions ("whether to go outside") from temporal
scheduling ("when to go outside"), they showed that point-forecast accuracy
metrics (MAE/RMSE) do not monotonically translate into optimal activity timing,
motivating our dual-track evaluation of both predictive error and conservative
decision validity.

C. Synthesis of Core Mindsets, Controversies, and Research Gap
Table I summarizes representative literature across AI methodology and domain
know-how. Synthesizing this body of work reveals five core domain mindsets that
govern AirGuard AI's design:
1) Exposure Context over Static Visualization: Concentration readings must be
contextualized by activity duration, time window, and population vulnerability,
while strictly distinguishing station concentration-time integrals from personal
inhaled dose [1], [9].
2) Spatiotemporal Fidelity and Provenance: Every data record must preserve
station identity, averaging window, unit, quality flag, and the distinction
among observed_at, published_at, and retrieved_at [5]-[8], [11].
3) Baseline-First and Leakage-Safe Validation: Persistence and Seasonal Naive
benchmarks under chronological and rolling-origin splits are mandatory prerequisites
before claiming ML improvements [12]-[14], [20].
4) Separation of Predictive Accuracy and Decision Utility: Lower average MAE
does not guarantee reliable window ranking when inter-window differences are
smaller than forecast uncertainty [15].
5) Constrained Agency and Fail-Closed Guardrails: LLMs should orchestrate intent
parsing and tool selection, whereas numeric calculations, AQI rule matching, and
provenance checks must be executed by deterministic, auditable code [16]-[18].

This synthesis also resolves three persistent controversies in the field:
1) Complex Deep Learning vs. Simple Baselines: While deep spatiotemporal networks
can capture nonlinear interactions in dense sensor grids, tree ensembles and
linear models often match or outperform them on single-station short-horizon
tasks with far greater reproducibility [12], [19], [20]; hence, AirGuard AI
mandates head-to-head validation against Persistence and Seasonal Naive before
selecting Ridge, Random Forest, or XGBoost.
2) Point-Forecast Error vs. Actionable Decision Quality: Because optimizing mean
squared error can still miss acute pollution spikes or over-rank nearly identical
windows [15], we evaluate both numerical forecasting skill (Track A) and
uncertainty-bounded window selection (Track B).
3) Autonomous LLM Agents vs. Rule-Constrained Tool Pipelines: To prevent prompt
injection and regulatory misrepresentation [17], [18], AirGuard AI restricts the
LLM to structured tool orchestration and enforces deterministic verification on
all final outputs.

Research Gap: Existing studies typically address PM2.5 forecasting, XAI visualization,
or conversational interfaces in isolation. There remains a lack of an end-to-end,
auditable framework tailored to Taiwan's official MOENV station network that
simultaneously enforces cryptographic data provenance, leakage-safe 1/3/6-hour
baseline benchmarking, versioned 2025 MOENV multi-pollutant AQI rule separation,
and fail-closed agent evaluation under incomplete or stale data conditions.

The specific responsibilities and testable outputs of each module are defined
as follows:
- Module [1] User / Web UI: Collects structured or natural language inputs
specifying monitoring station selection (via interactive TWD97 station map),
activity type, planned duration, population category (general vs. sensitive),
and candidate time window.
- Module [2] Query Interpreter: Parses user requests into the canonical JSON
schema (query.schema.json), standardizing timestamps to Asia/Taipei (UTC+8) for
display and UTC for internal computation. If essential fields are missing or a
station name is ambiguous, it halts execution and requests user clarification.
- Module [3] Constrained Planner Agent: Executes a tool-allowlisted reasoning
loop. The agent is prohibited from performing free-form arithmetic or generating
unverified environmental readings; it can only invoke eight registered tools
with strict schema validation and bounded retry limits.
- Module [4] MOENV Data Acquisition & Quality Gate: Interfaces with the MOENV
REST API v2 (https://data.moenv.gov.tw/api/v2/{DATASET}) using server-side API
key resolution (MOENV_API_KEY) or verified offline snapshots. It audits unit
consistency (ug/m^3), duplicate records, missingness flags, and freshness latency.
- Module [5] PM2.5 Forecast & Analytics Engine: Constructs leakage-free lagged
and rolling features available at forecast origin t, generates h in {1, 3, 6}-hour
PM2.5 concentration predictions (ug/m^3) from version-locked models, attaches
validation error references, and optionally computes P1 SHAP feature attributions.
- Module [6] Versioned Official AQI Domain Rules: Encodes Taiwan MOENV's
official AQI tiers (0-50 Good, 51-100 Moderate, 101-150 Unhealthy for Sensitive
Groups, 151-200 Unhealthy for All, 201-300 Very Unhealthy, 301-500 Hazardous)
and corresponding guidance for general and sensitive populations, recording the
effective regulatory version (2025 revision) [11].
- Module [7] Conservative Activity Window Comparator: Evaluates candidate
activity intervals within the 6-hour horizon, computes the station-scale
concentration-time exposure proxy, and applies an uncertainty-overlap rule that
declines to force a ranking when candidate windows are statistically
indistinguishable relative to validation error.
- Module [8] Evidence-Grounded Output & Audit Logger: Enforces final output
verification (report.schema.json), displaying official AQI, predicted hourly
PM2.5, and exposure proxy in distinct visual cards with explicit provenance
URLs, timestamps, scope disclaimers, and a redacted audit trace.

B. Official Open Data Contracts and Temporal Semantics
Table II summarizes the four official MOENV datasets integrated into AirGuard AI.
To prevent silent semantic drift across modules, all internal data exchanges
adhere to five formal data contracts:

1) Observation: {station_id, pollutant, value, unit, observed_at_utc,
published_at_utc?, retrieved_at_utc, source_id, source_url, quality_flag in
{ok, missing, invalid}}.
2) OfficialAQI: {station_id, official_aqi, status_category, dominant_pollutant,
published_at_utc?, retrieved_at_utc, source_id="AQX_P_432", source_url}.
3) Forecast: {station_id, origin_at_utc, target_at_utc, horizon_hours in {1,3,6},
pm25_ug_m3, model_version, dataset_version, error_reference_mae, interval?}.
4) ActivityWindow: {station_id, start_at_utc, duration_hours, population_group
in {general, sensitive, unspecified}, activity_type, concentration_source,
valid_coverage_ratio, exposure_index_ug_h_m3?}.
5) AgentResponse: {status in {ok, partial, needs_input, unavailable,
out_of_horizon}, interpreted_request, official_aqi?, forecasts[],
considered_windows[], recommendation?, sources[], limitations[], generated_at,
trace_id}.

A critical engineering rule in our data contract is that published_at_utc must
never be fabricated from retrieved_at_utc. When historical datasets (AQX_P_13
and AQX_P_488) provide only the observation timestamp (monitordate + hour or
datacreationdate) without historical publication latency logs, published_at_utc
is explicitly stored as null, and any resulting forecasting backtest is tagged
with backtest_mode = "reconstructed_from_observed_at".

C. Leakage-Safe Short-Term PM2.5 Forecasting (Track A Methodology)
Let y_{s,t} denote the raw hourly observed PM2.5 concentration (in ug/m^3) at
monitoring station s at discrete hour t. The forecasting task is to estimate
y_hat_{s, t+h|t} for forecast horizons h in {1, 3, 6} hours using exclusively
information available up to origin t.

1) Causal Feature Engineering: For each station s and origin t, the feature
vector x_{s,t} is constructed strictly from historical observations {y_{s, t-l}}
with lag indices l in {1, 3, 6, 12, 24} hours, causal backward rolling window
statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum over the preceding
3, 6, and 24 hours computed over [t-w+1, t]), cyclical calendar encodings
(sin/cos transformations of hour-of-day and day-of-week), binary missing-data
indicator flags, and verified co-located wind speed/direction features when
available at time t. No forward filling across future timestamps or global
imputation prior to temporal splitting is permitted.

2) Mandatory Naive Baselines: Every candidate ML model is benchmarked against
two parameter-free baselines evaluated on the exact same valid sample pairs:
   - Persistence Baseline:
       y_hat_pers(t + h | t) = y(t)                                          (1)
   - 24-Hour Seasonal Naive Baseline:
       y_hat_snaive(t + h | t) = y(t + h - 24)                               (2)
     where t + h - 24 <= t for all h in {1, 3, 6}, ensuring strict causality.

3) Candidate Machine Learning Models: We evaluate three interpretable and
computationally efficient tabular regression families suitable for station-scale
deployment without GPU dependencies: L2-regularized linear regression (Ridge),
Random Forest (RF), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). In accordance with
our baseline-first mindset, complex deep sequence architectures (e.g., LSTM or
Transformers) are not assumed superior a priori.

4) Chronological Splitting, Rolling-Origin Validation, and Metrics: Each
station's chronological series is partitioned into approximately 70% training,
15% validation, and 15% held-out testing segments. Hyperparameter tuning and
feature selection are conducted using rolling-origin forward validation across
the development partitions, while the held-out test set is evaluated exactly
once after model freezing. For a given station s and horizon h over the set of
valid target pairs N_{s,h} (where both features at t and ground truth at t+h
have quality_flag == "ok"), we report:

   MAE_{s,h}  = (1 / |N_{s,h}|) * sum_{t in N_{s,h}} |y_{s,t+h} - y_hat_{s,t+h|t}|   (3)

   RMSE_{s,h} = sqrt( (1 / |N_{s,h}|) * sum_{t in N_{s,h}} (y_{s,t+h} - y_hat_{s,t+h|t})^2 ) (4)

   Skill_MAE(s, h) = 1 - ( MAE_model(s, h) / MAE_pers(s, h) )                        (5)

where Skill_MAE > 0 indicates a reduction in mean absolute error relative to
the Persistence baseline (and returns N/A if MAE_pers == 0). Models are further
stratified by station, horizon, season, and high-pollution episodes. If a
trained ML model fails to achieve positive skill over Persistence on validation
data, the system transparently reports the negative result and defaults to
baseline/trend reporting rather than fabricating improvements. Algorithm 1
formalizes the leakage-safe training and evaluation protocol.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Algorithm 1: Leakage-Safe Multi-Horizon PM2.5 Training and Backtesting
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Input : Raw station hourly series D_raw, horizons H = {1, 3, 6}, split ratios
        (0.70, 0.15, 0.15), duplicate policy on_duplicate = "dedup_identical"
Output: Versioned frozen models M_frozen, stratified evaluation report R_eval
01: D <- StandardizeUnitsAndTimezones(D_raw, unit="ug/m^3", tz="UTC")
02: D <- ReconcileDuplicatesAndFlagQC(D, on_duplicate, max_valid_pm25=1000.0)
03: D <- SortChronologicallyByStationAndTime(D)
04: for each station s in Stations(D) do
05:   for each horizon h in H do
06:     (X_{s,h}, y_{s,h}, mask_valid) <- BuildCausalLagRollingPairs(D_s, cutoff=t, target=t+h)
07:     (Train, Valid, Test) <- ChronologicalSplit(X_{s,h}, y_{s,h}, mask_valid, 0.70, 0.15, 0.15)
08:     pred_pers <- PredictPersistence(Valid, Test, h)
09:     pred_snaive <- PredictSeasonalNaive24h(Valid, Test, h)
10:     Candidates <- {Ridge, RandomForest, XGBoost}
11:     for each model m in Candidates do
12:       FitPreprocessorsOnTrainOnly(m, Train)
13:       ScoreRollingOrigin(m, Train, Valid, metrics={MAE, RMSE, Skill_MAE})
14:     end for
15:     m_best <- SelectByValidMAEAndLatency(Candidates, pred_pers, pred_snaive)
16:     FreezeModelAndVersion(m_best, dataset_sha256=Hash(D_s))
17:     pred_test <- EvaluateOnceOnHeldOutTest(m_best, Test)
18:     CalibrateResidualErrorBands(m_best, Valid)
19:     R_eval[s, h] <- StratifyMetricsByEpisodeAndSeason(pred_test, pred_pers, pred_snaive)
20:     AnnotateBacktestMode(R_eval[s, h], mode="reconstructed_from_observed_at")
21:   end for
22: end for
23: return M_frozen, R_eval
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

D. Tool-Allowlisted Agent, Exposure Proxy, and Fail-Closed Decision Logic
To translate station observations and short-term forecasts into reliable
activity guidance, Module [3] implements an evidence-grounded planner agent
restricted to eight deterministic tools:
1) list_stations(county): Retrieves official MOENV station IDs, names, types,
   and TWD97 coordinates from AQX_P_07 (prohibited from inferring street-level
   pollution hotspots).
2) get_official_aqi(station_id): Fetches the latest official AQI, dominant
   pollutant, and published_at timestamp from AQX_P_432 (prohibited from
   presenting cached records as live data).
3) get_pm25_history(station_id, window_hours): Retrieves quality-checked hourly
   PM2.5 series and coverage ratios from AQX_P_13 (prohibited from zero-filling
   missing hours).
4) forecast_pm25(station_id, horizons): Invokes frozen models for h in {1, 3, 6}
   hours and returns point predictions with validation error references
   (prohibited from extrapolating beyond 6 hours).
5) get_official_guidance(aqi_value, population_group): Looks up versioned MOENV
   activity guidance for general or sensitive groups (prohibited from altering
   regulatory breakpoints).
6) compare_windows(candidate_windows, forecasts, error_margin): Compares
   eligible candidate windows within the 6-hour horizon when data coverage is
   valid (prohibited from guaranteeing clinical health safety).
7) estimate_exposure_index(window, concentrations): Computes the deterministic
   station-scale concentration-time integral (prohibited from claiming personal
   inhaled dose).
8) verify_output(response_payload): Audits numerical fidelity, unit presence,
   timestamp alignment, citation completeness, and safety disclaimers prior to
   user delivery.

For any candidate outdoor activity window W partitioned into sub-intervals i
with duration Delta_t_i (in hours) and verified station or model PM2.5
concentration C_s(t_i) (in ug/m^3), the station-scale concentration-time
exposure index is defined as:

   ExposureIndex(W) = sum_{i in W} C_s(t_i) * Delta_t_i    [ug*h/m^3]       (6)

We explicitly enforce three domain guardrails on Eq. (6): First, ExposureIndex(W)
is an ambient station-scale exposure proxy in ug*h/m^3, not an individual's
inhaled microgram dose (which would require minute ventilation and microenvironment
infiltration modeling [9]). Second, if any sub-interval i within W lacks a valid
concentration reading, the system reports the valid coverage ratio and marks
unobserved portions as unknown rather than substituting 0 ug/m^3. Third, when
comparing two candidate windows W_1 and W_2 whose predicted mean concentrations
differ by less than the model's validation error reference (|C_hat(W_1) -
C_hat(W_2)| < tau_err), the comparator abstains from issuing a high-confidence
ranking, informing the user that the candidate intervals fall within predictive
uncertainty. Algorithm 2 details the complete runtime execution and fail-closed
verification loop of the AirGuard AI agent.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Algorithm 2: Evidence-Grounded AirGuard Decision Agent (Runtime Execution)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Input : Natural language query Q, user context C_ctx, allowlisted tool registry T_allow
Output: Schema-validated AgentResponse R_out with audit trace
01: req <- ParseQueryIntent(Q, C_ctx, tz="Asia/Taipei")
02: if MissingRequiredFields(req, required={location, time_window, group}) then
03:   return ComposeResponse(status="needs_input", prompt=AskClarification(req))
04: end if
05: station <- InvokeTool(T_allow.list_stations, req.location)
06: if station is ambiguous or null then
07:   return ComposeResponse(status="needs_input", prompt=ListCandidateStations(req.location))
08: end if
09: horizons <- ValidateRequestedHorizon(req.time_window, allowed={1, 3, 6}, max_h=6)
10: if horizons is unsupported then
11:   return ComposeResponse(status="out_of_horizon", limitations=Explain6hBoundary())
12: end if
13: live_aqi <- InvokeTool(T_allow.get_official_aqi, station.station_id)
14: hist_pm25 <- InvokeTool(T_allow.get_pm25_history, station.station_id, window_hours=24)
15: qc <- ValidateSourceTimestampUnitAndCoverage(live_aqi, hist_pm25)
16: if qc.is_stale or qc.coverage_insufficient then
17:   return SafeFallbackWithProvenance(status="partial_or_unavailable", qc=qc, live=live_aqi)
18: end if
19: forecasts <- InvokeTool(T_allow.forecast_pm25, station.station_id, horizons={1, 3, 6})
20: guidance <- InvokeTool(T_allow.get_official_guidance, live_aqi.official_aqi, req.group)
21: windows <- InvokeTool(T_allow.compare_windows, req.time_window, forecasts, guidance)
22: exposure <- InvokeTool(T_allow.estimate_exposure_index, windows, req.duration_hours)
23: R_draft <- AssembleSeparateCards(official=live_aqi, predicted=forecasts,
                                     exposure=exposure, guidance=guidance, qc=qc)
24: audit <- InvokeTool(T_allow.verify_output, R_draft)
25: if not audit.passed then
26:   R_out <- SafeFallbackWithProvenance(status="partial", qc=qc, live=live_aqi)
27: else
28:   R_out <- R_draft
29: end if
30: SaveRedactedAuditTrace(req, R_out, audit.trace_id)
31: return R_out
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Crucially, the Phase W1 empirical audit exposed five real-world data anomalies
in the upstream government API that would silently corrupt standard ML pipelines
if left unaddressed:
1) Upstream Duplicate Daily JSON Objects: Within the first 1,000 daily records
fetched per general station from AQX_P_13, exactly 21 dates (504 hourly slots,
frequently occurring on month-end dates such as 2026-01-31, 2026-02-28, and
2026-03-31) contained 2 to 4 identical duplicate JSON rows. AirGuard AI's
default policy (on_duplicate="error") blocks silent ingestion, while batch
normalization (on_duplicate="dedup_identical") verifies that all 24 hourly values
and quality flags match identically before deduplicating (verified conflict count
= 0).
2) Unobserved Calendar Days and Trailing Whitespace: In AQX_P_13, the
monitordate field contains trailing whitespace (e.g., "2026-11-23 "), and out
of 988 calendar days between 2024-03-11 and 2026-11-23, exactly 9 calendar days
(216 hours, including one day each in May, June, and July 2026) were entirely
absent from the upstream API response. Our quality auditor explicitly tracks
these in unobserved_hours and max_consecutive_missing_hours (reaching 73h-99h)
without interpolating or zero-filling across gaps.
3) Instrument Quality-Control Thresholding: While Taiwan's AQI 500 breakpoint
for PM2.5 corresponds to 500.4 ug/m^3 [11], beta-attenuation and Federal
Equivalent Method (FEM) monitors deployed at MOENV stations operate up to a
physical ceiling of approximately 1,000 ug/m^3. We configure a transparent,
auditable threshold MAX_VALID_PM25_UG_M3 = 1000.0 ug/m^3 (recorded in
qc_thresholds) so extreme pollution episodes above 500.4 ug/m^3 are not silently
clipped while transmission spikes above 1,000 ug/m^3 are flagged as invalid.
4) Backend Pagination Scan Timeout: Unfiltered AQX_P_13 queries span ~80 stations
x 18 items (~1,440 daily rows per day). With server-side filters (siteid and
itemengname), the first two pages (offset=0 and offset=500, limit=500, covering
~2.7 years) return in ~1.3 s, whereas offset >= 1000 triggers a >20 s backend
table-scan timeout on the MOENV server, motivating our hybrid live-page snapshot
and offline archive replay architecture.
5) Cryptographic Offline Reproducibility: Every live fetch writes raw unmodified
page bytes (page_0000_offset_0.json, page_0001_offset_500.json) and a key-redacted
manifest.json recording page and batch SHA256 digests. Offline replay via
load_raw_snapshot() re-verifies every byte digest before recomputing identical
quality reports.

B. Dual-Track Evaluation Protocol for Phases W2-W6
To evaluate the predictive models and the Domain AI Agent without conflating
numerical error with decision reliability, we establish a two-track validation
protocol (Table V):

- Track A (Multi-Horizon PM2.5 Forecasting & Decision Loss Replay): Using the
verified 33-month hourly histories of the Taichung stations (with Xitun Station
ID 32 serving as the primary pilot station and Stations 28-31 and 88 providing
cross-station generalization checks), Track A evaluates Persistence, 24-hour
Seasonal Naive, Ridge, Random Forest, and XGBoost at h in {1, 3, 6} hours under
Algorithm 1. Beyond overall and episode-stratified MAE, RMSE, and Skill_MAE,
Track A replays historical candidate windows on the held-out set to measure
decision regret—defined as the station-scale ExposureIndex difference (ug*h/m^3)
between the model-selected window and the ex-post minimum-concentration observed
window—alongside the proportion of queries triggering uncertainty abstention.

- Track B (30-Scenario Agent & Guardrail Benchmark): Track B evaluates the
end-to-end agent against a fixed casebook of at least 30 test scenarios (6
categories x 5 cases, detailed in Table V) under identical data snapshots,
comparing a deterministic Rule-Only baseline against the full AirGuard AI
(Agent + ML + Domain Rules) pipeline across five quantitative metrics: 1) Tool
Selection Accuracy (%); 2) Provenance & Citation Completeness Rate (%);
3) Official MOENV Rule Consistency Rate (%); 4) Fail-Closed Safe Degradation
Rate (%); and 5) End-to-End Response Latency (Median P50 and P95 in ms).

C. Scope Refinement and Scientific Integrity Statement
During the transition from system specification v2 to v3 (documented on November
24, 2026), we conducted a formal feasibility audit against the empirical MOENV
data schemas and excluded street-level clean-route ranking and personal inhaled
dose estimation from core deliverables, as point-source regulatory stations
cannot scientifically validate street-canyon exposure differences without
independent mobile monitoring [9], [10]. Furthermore, in strict compliance with
research integrity standards, we emphasize that while Phase W1 data ingestion,
quality auditing across the six Taichung stations, and 19 offline CI unit tests
are fully implemented and empirically verified (Tables III and IV), Phase W2
baseline backtesting (AirGuard-AI Issue #10) and Phase W3-W6 ML/Agent empirical
scoring are currently in active execution; therefore, no unverified model
MAE/RMSE figures or agent pass rates are pre-claimed in this manuscript.


V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

This paper presented the design, methodology, and Phase W1 empirical data
verification of AirGuard AI, an evidence-grounded Domain AI Agent for station-
scale short-term PM2.5 forecasting and conservative outdoor activity decision
support. By integrating four official Taiwan MOENV open datasets (AQX_P_07,
AQX_P_432, AQX_P_488, and AQX_P_13) with a leakage-safe 1/3/6-hour forecasting
protocol, versioned 2025 MOENV AQI activity guidelines, and an eight-tool
allowlisted ReAct planner agent, AirGuard AI addresses the critical translation
gap between raw environmental monitoring tables and trustworthy personal
activity scheduling. Our Phase W1 empirical audit across all six official
monitoring stations in Taichung City (covering 123,055 valid hourly PM2.5
observations from 2024 to 2026 with cryptographic SHA256 snapshot reproducibility)
demonstrated high data completeness (95.49%-97.90% window coverage) while
identifying and resolving upstream duplicate daily records, unobserved calendar
days, and API pagination timeout boundaries.

In ongoing and future work across Phases W2-W6, we are executing the pre-registered
Track A rolling-origin backtests comparing Persistence, Seasonal Naive, Ridge,
Random Forest, and XGBoost across all six Taichung stations, followed by Track B
empirical benchmarking across the 30 adversarial and boundary scenarios,
integration of P1 SHAP tree explainability and multi-activity exposure logging,
and open-source release of the interactive FastAPI/Next.js decision interface.
